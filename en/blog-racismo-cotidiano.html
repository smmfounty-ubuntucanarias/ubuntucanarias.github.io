When racism passes for normal

Soraya Mohamedi · Psychology graduate and transpersonal therapist · Employment Area Coordinator, Ubuntu Canarias · 8 min read · Published 5 November 2026

Everyday phrases that seem harmless, the prejudice behind each one and what you can say in reply.

When we think of racism, what comes to mind is insults, threats or someone who is not allowed into a place. That exists. But there is another kind of racism that goes more unnoticed, because it comes wrapped in ordinary phrases, sometimes even friendly ones.

It turns up in a conversation at a bar, in a job interview, in the queue at the health centre or from someone who insists they have no prejudice at all. These are learned ideas about what people from a certain country, religion or skin colour are like, and they shape how we see and treat them.

Sometimes they take the form of a question. Other times, a comparison or something that sounds like a compliment. What matters is not the tone, but what the phrase takes for granted.

“Well, you’re different”

Imagine someone says to you: “Immigrants are freeloaders, they don’t want to work… Well, you’re different.”

They may say it with a smile and with no wish to offend, and it sounds like a compliment. But look at what they have just done: first they spoke badly about a whole group, and then they took you out of it, as if to earn their respect you had to prove you are not like the others.

This kind of prejudice usually leaves some signs:

  • Generalising: “they’re all the same”, “those people are like that”, “they come here to take advantage”.
  • Making an exception for you: “you’re different”, “you don’t look like you’re from there”, “you really have integrated”.
  • Comparing: “you’re polite, not like the rest”.
  • Doubting that you belong here: “but where are you really from?”, even after you have answered.
  • Praising you because you don’t fit the idea that person has of your group.

Often the person saying it doesn’t even realise. But not meaning to hurt doesn’t mean the comment doesn’t hurt.

What can you say without ending up in an argument? Something simple: “What do you mean, I’m different?”, “I don’t need to be an exception for you to treat me with respect” or “I think you’re generalising. Everyone has their own story.”

You can also choose not to answer. Some days you have no energy, you don’t feel safe or you simply don’t want to have that conversation. Choosing when, how and with whom to talk is also setting a limit.

And if you do answer, you don’t have to justify yourself or prove you are a good person. You can point out a comment without insulting or humiliating the person who made it.

Phrases you often hear and what you can say

There is no perfect answer. It depends on who says it, where you are and how you feel that day. Here are some ideas, in case they help.

“But where are you really from?”

Someone asks where you are from, you say from here, and that’s not enough. They insist because your face or your surname doesn’t match what they expected.

You can answer: “I’m from here. Why is that hard to believe?” And if you feel like talking about your family, go ahead, but because you want to, not because you have to justify yourself.

“If you come here, adapt to our customs”

Living together means knowing and respecting some shared rules, and that applies to everyone. It is something else to ask someone to stop speaking their language, eating what they eat or celebrating their festivals.

One possible answer: “Following the rules doesn’t mean I stop being who I am.”

“Immigrants come here to take advantage of benefits”

This attributes to millions of people the intention of taking advantage. Benefits have requirements and are processed the same way for everyone.

You can ask: “Have you seen that in any figures, or did you just hear it somewhere?” Or turn it around: “When a Spanish person receives a benefit, do you think the same about all Spanish people?”

“You speak very good Spanish for a foreigner”

It sounds like a compliment, but it starts from the idea that the normal thing would be for you to speak it badly.

Sometimes a simple “Thank you. Why does that surprise you?” is enough.

“You don’t look like an immigrant”

It assumes immigrants have a particular face. There are migrants of every colour, age and profession.

The question that exposes it is simple: “And what should I look like?”

“I’m not racist, I have foreign friends”

Having friends from other countries is good, but it doesn’t protect anyone from prejudice. We all have some.

You can say: “I’m not saying you’re racist. I’m saying that comment is.” Separating the person from the phrase helps them not to get defensive.

“That’s just how they are”

Someone has a bad experience with one person and turns it into a feature of a whole country, religion or culture.

A useful question: “Are you talking about someone in particular or about all of them?” You can criticise what one person did without blaming their whole people.

“You should be grateful they gave you a chance”

You hear this a lot at work and when looking for housing. It leaves the person permanently in debt, as if a contract or a rental were a favour.

Being grateful is fine. What it doesn’t mean is accepting fewer rights. You can say: “I’m grateful, and I have the same rights as any other worker.”

If you witness it and it isn’t aimed at you

It isn’t always easy to react. You may be afraid of causing a conflict, looking bad in front of others or, if it happens at work, having problems with your job. It’s normal to hesitate.

There are ways to step in that don’t require a confrontation:

  • Ask. “What do you mean by that?” makes the other person explain their idea, and sometimes they realise on their own what they have said.
  • Say you don’t think it’s right. “I think that comment is unfair” is enough. No speech needed.
  • Go over to the person it was aimed at. Ask how they are and don’t leave them alone. Knowing that someone else saw it too can change a lot about how it feels.
  • Keep evidence if it happens at work, when looking for housing, at a school or in a public service: messages, dates, names of who was there. With that you can ask a specialised organisation for advice.
  • Look after yourself. If the person is aggressive, nobody is obliged to confront them at that moment. You can ask for help or act later.

Looking at our own prejudices too

Prejudices are not always conscious. We learn them at home, in the neighbourhood, on TV or from a bad experience we end up generalising. It happens to everyone, including people who have migrated, towards other groups.

Some questions that help you check yourself:

  • Am I judging this person for what they did or for where they come from?
  • Does it surprise me that someone from another country is a doctor, an engineer or a teacher?
  • Do I think I know a culture because I have dealt with two or three people from there?
  • Am I confusing a different custom with bad behaviour?

Noticing a prejudice doesn’t make you a bad person. It gives you the chance to change it.

Respect isn’t earned by being the exception

Many migrants are asked, without anyone saying so, to be especially hard-working, grateful, discreet and polite in order to deserve fair treatment. Nobody else is asked for the same.

Rights are not a reward for good behaviour. And living together works when respect goes both ways.

Working against racism also means learning to see these small phrases which, repeated every day, make someone feel like an outsider. At Ubuntu Canarias we want to open spaces to talk about these experiences, recognise them and look together for ways to face them. If this text helps you spot one of them, or know what to say next time, that is already a step.
