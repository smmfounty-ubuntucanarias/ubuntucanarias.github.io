I no longer live waiting to be rejected

Soraya Mohamedi · Psychology graduate and transpersonal therapist · Employment Area Coordinator, Ubuntu Canarias · 9 min read · Published 16 November 2026

For years I lived expecting rejection. What I learned about identity, fear and prejudice, including my own, and why I believe the work against racism involves all of us.

As a child I learned very early that my origin could become a reason for rejection. My family is of North African origin and I grew up in Spain hearing comments and living through situations that made me feel that, for some people, I did not fully belong to a place that was also mine.

I remember there were families who did not want their children to come to my birthday parties because they thought it was dangerous to mix with «moros». I also remember something that stayed with me for a long time: whenever I argued with someone, stood up for my rights or simply expressed an opinion they did not like, some people stopped seeing me as Spanish. Suddenly, my origin became an argument to discredit me.

Over the years, even the label people gave me changed. When my appearance led some people to mistake me for a Latin American woman, the comments changed too. And when they heard my Canarian accent and assumed I was Spanish, I found myself in situations where people spoke quite calmly in front of me about «estos panchitos mafiosos», «estos negratas invasores», «estos moros conflictivos» or «estos chinos abusadores»: insults aimed at Latin Americans, Black people, North Africans and Chinese people.

That made me think a great deal: sometimes the way we are treated depends on the identity others assign to us. And that identity can change in a matter of seconds, without us having changed at all.

For a long time I was very alert to those signals. I had learned to anticipate rejection. If someone made a comment, I might wonder whether there was a racist intention behind it. If someone looked at me in a certain way, I might read it through all that accumulated experience. I had internalised a warning I heard many times: «They will be racist to you».

Over the years, however, I also had to look inside myself.

What I have learned accompanying other people

Working with migrant, bicultural and Spanish people, accompanying personal and social processes, I have heard many stories, come to know very different realities and been able to observe closely how we relate to our identity, to fear, to belonging and to the need to be recognised.

There is something that repeats itself, beyond nationalities and cultures: we all need to feel that we belong, that we matter and that someone recognises that we exist.

Identity is an essential part of our psychological health. We need to know who we are, know our history and feel that we have a place in the world. It is a kind of backbone that helps us build ourselves and relate to others.

But that same need to belong can become a problem when we feel our identity is threatened. Sometimes, to protect what we believe we are, we put up borders, look for differences, need to feel superior or reject what we do not know.

I have also seen that people can reproduce prejudices they have learned without ever questioning them. Sometimes we repeat phrases, ideas and behaviours because they are part of our surroundings, because we want to fit in or because we need a group's approval. That does not automatically make a person incapable of change, but it does remind us how important it is to review what we think and do.

And that review is my job too. It would not be honest to talk about other people's prejudices without acknowledging that we can all have them, even when we believe we are far removed from them.

What happens when we need to feel above someone?

Racism has no single explanation. It has historical, social, economic and political dimensions, and it is also linked to power relations and privilege. We cannot reduce it to an individual problem or explain all its forms through psychology.

But understanding some psychological mechanisms can help us see why one person comes to despise another because of where they come from.

Sometimes there is fear of losing a place, a position or recognition. At other times there is the need to feel superior, to find someone to blame for one's own problems or to strengthen one's belonging to a group. Insecurity, social comparison, upbringing, media manipulation and narratives that present certain groups as a threat also play a part.

Some people need to feel above others to make up for a sense of inferiority or to gain recognition they cannot find any other way. Others repeat inherited ideas without stopping to think about their consequences. And there are also those who knowingly use fear and division to serve particular interests.

Understanding these mechanisms does not justify discriminatory behaviour. What I am looking for is to reach their roots so we can work on them. If we do not understand how prejudice is built, we will hardly be able to change the conditions that feed it.

When we stop reading the world through fear

Today something happens to me that years ago I would have found hard to imagine: I interpret situations less and less from the expectation of finding racism.

Racism has not disappeared; I recognise it and I know how to identify discrimination when it happens. I know it exists and I know its consequences. What has changed is the way I observe and respond.

Before, I could find racism in many gestures because I had learned to be on guard. Now I try to stop and look at what is happening. Sometimes I find ignorance, fear, a need for recognition, a desire to feel superior, inherited prejudice, political interests or media manipulation. At other times, of course, I find direct discrimination.

This difference matters. Not every situation has the same origin or deserves the same response. Nor can we ask someone who has suffered discrimination to constantly doubt their own experience. Everyone has their own story and their own reasons for interpreting what they live through.

What I have learned is that living permanently on the defensive can end up taking up too much space in our lives. Constant vigilance is exhausting. It can lead us to anticipate conflicts, to read every encounter through an old wound and to hand other people's opinions a power over our identity that they should not have.

We cannot control what other people think, say or do. Nor can we avoid every experience of rejection. But we can work so that someone's contempt does not become the measure of our worth.

That does not take the pain away. Discriminatory words and actions can cause real emotional, social and material harm. But we can learn to protect our identity, know our rights, set limits and decide how to respond without letting another person's prejudice end up defining the way we live.

A task that involves all of us

At Ubuntu Canarias we believe racism needs to be understood and addressed from different places. We do not want to build our work on permanent confrontation or make pointing fingers our main tool. We want to get to the causes, open spaces for reflection, educate and help people review their own ideas and behaviour.

There is an image that captures how I see this work: it is a task for two teams, the one that has the ball and the one that receives the throw.

In that relationship the responsibilities are different. Whoever discriminates is responsible for their behaviour and must answer for it. Whoever suffers discrimination is not to blame for what happens and is not obliged to put up with it, justify it or educate their aggressor. But they can find tools to protect themselves, strengthen their identity and stop other people's contempt from becoming a truth about themselves.

And there is a third space we must not forget: that of the society that watches, passes on, reproduces or questions these behaviours. Families, schools, the media, institutions, associations and shared community spaces also have a lot to do.

That is why we speak of shared responsibility, but not identical responsibility. Each person and each institution has a different part to play in building a society where origin, culture or appearance do not determine anyone's worth or opportunities.

At Ubuntu Canarias we want to work from understanding, education, mutual knowledge and awareness. We want people to be able to recognise their prejudices, understand where they come from and question them. We also want to accompany those who have experienced rejection so they can rebuild a sense of safety, belonging and trust.

We do not want anyone to have to live waiting to be rejected. We want to help each person know their worth, understand human diversity and build relationships based on respect.

Today I am in a different place

Today my identity is much firmer. I no longer need to prove that I belong, explain my origin or seek the approval of those who cannot recognise me. I do not need anyone to consider me Spanish to know who I am, or anyone to accept my story to feel whole.

I am interested in humanity in all its diversity: people, their cultures, their stories, their contradictions and their ways of understanding the world. I am interested in learning from what is different and recognising what we share.

I have also learned that I do not want to live against people. I want to live with them.

That does not oblige me to accept any behaviour or to give up defending rights. What I do not want is for what I reject to end up governing the way I look at the world. I can recognise an injustice, set limits and act against it without turning my whole life into a response to other people's prejudice.

I believe we need more spaces where we can meet and truly get to know each other. More education and critical thinking. More ability to review our own ideas and less need to place ourselves permanently on opposing sides.

For me, change began when I was able to look inside myself as well. When I stopped taking for granted all the ideas I had inherited. When I acknowledged my fears and understood that no one needs to be inferior for me to feel worthy.

I do not want racism to determine how I look at humanity. I want the way I look at humanity to be bigger than racism.
