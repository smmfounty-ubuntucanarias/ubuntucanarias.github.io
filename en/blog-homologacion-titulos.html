Getting a foreign qualification recognised in Spain: what no one explains at the start

Ubuntu Canarias Team · Socio-labour guidance

Someone who worked as a nurse for ten years back home, or who holds a master's degree in engineering, arrives here and runs into an uncomfortable question: is that qualification worth anything, or do you have to start from zero? The short answer is “it depends”, and that short answer is exactly what causes the most anxiety, because no one explains well what it depends on.

Four procedures that get confused under the same word

“Homologación” (recognition) has become the catch-all term for “making my qualification count here”, but there are actually four different procedures, and mixing them up wastes time and, sometimes, money on the wrong one.

The question to ask before starting any procedure

It's not “can I get my qualification recognised?”, it's “do I actually need it recognised for what I want to do?”. That's where many people lose months.

One fact that surprises many people: you don't need a work permit in Spain, or even to be living here, to start the recognition procedure. If the qualification is official in your home country, you can start the process from abroad, even before migrating.

On the other hand, not everything can be recognised. Continuing-education diplomas, qualifications that aren't an official bachelor's or master's degree, ones that already received a negative decision, and those obtained mainly through recognition of work experience rather than formal study are all excluded. And if you're already processing a convalidación of those studies directly with a Spanish university, you don't also need to go through homologación — the two don't combine.

If your goal is to work in a regulated profession — teaching at a public school, practising medicine, nursing, law — you do need full homologación, no shortcuts. If your goal is a non-regulated job, it's often better to start job-hunting with the unrecognised qualification, explaining it clearly at interview, and leave the recognition process running in parallel rather than treating it as a prerequisite. Waiting for the qualification to be recognised before job-hunting, when the job doesn't actually require it, is one of the most common and most time-costly mistakes.

Which professions require full homologación

Not every degree requires the long procedure. Regulated professions — the ones that require homologación if the qualification is from outside the EU — are concentrated in a few specific sectors: health (medicine, nursing, physiotherapy, dentistry, pharmacy, health psychology, among others), higher and technical engineering and architecture, teaching (from early years through vocational training), and the legal field (lawyers and court representatives). This list is set out in the annex to Royal Decree 889/2022.

One important nuance for anyone coming from abroad with a law degree: for the legal profession, getting the qualification recognised isn't the last step. Afterwards you still need to complete the Master's in Access to the Legal Profession and pass the corresponding state exam before you can practise.

If what you want recognised isn't a university degree

Everything above — the Ministry of Science, Innovation and Universities, the €166.50 fee — applies to bachelor's, master's or other degree-level qualifications. If what you completed in your home country was secondary education or the equivalent of A-levels/high school, the procedure is handled by the Ministry of Education, Vocational Training and Sport, with general information on its official website.

If it's specifically a Vocational Training (FP) qualification, and the goal is to access FP studies in Spain by presenting that foreign qualification, it needs to be recognised first through that same Ministry's electronic office. For any doubts about the procedure, the Ministry runs its own citizen support service.

If you live in the Canary Islands, there's also a separate procedure run by the Government of the Canary Islands for recognising the equivalence of a qualification or completed studies, when that equivalence needs an individual decision. Unlike university homologación, this procedure has no fee, can be submitted year-round, and allows two routes:

  • Online, with the application form, ID document and academic qualification or transcript.
  • In person, at any registry office of the Government of the Canary Islands, with the same documents.

One important clarification: this recognition gives the same academic or professional effects as the qualification it's compared to, but does not grant the Spanish qualification itself.

What the university procedure actually asks for

Since November 2022, recognition of foreign university qualifications is handled exclusively through the electronic office of the Ministry of Science, Innovation and Universities. It isn't done on paper, and it isn't done over the counter.

The documents you need to upload, with their exact name in the system:

  • IDENTIDAD — your ID document (usually a passport).
  • TITULO — the original qualification, legalised or apostilled if applicable.
  • TRADTITULO — a certified translation of the qualification, if it isn't in Spanish.
  • CAO — the official academic transcript (subjects completed), or the Diploma Supplement if you have one instead.
  • TRADCAO — a certified translation of that transcript, if it isn't in Spanish.
  • LINGU — proof of Spanish level, required only for homologación (not for equivalencia).
  • TASA — proof of fee payment.

On legalising the original qualification, there are two valid routes: through the education authorities of the country where it was obtained, or through the Spanish consular office in that same country. Either one works.

A practical detail: although the technical limit is 10 MB per file, it's best not to go over 5 MB, to avoid upload problems.

The fee costs €166.50 and can be paid in four ways: with form 790 printed and paid at a Spanish bank, by transfer to a Bank of Spain account enabled for payments from abroad, online by card or account, or waived if you're a beneficiary of temporary protection.

On access: you need to identify yourself with Cl@ve (if you have a NIF or NIE) or, if you don't have those, create a user account with your passport or other documentation from your home country. A curious detail: if the system throws an error when creating the account because the passport number “matches the format” of a NIF or NIE, simply adding three zeros before the number avoids the conflict.

The application can be saved as a draft and picked up again later — although uploaded documents aren't kept between sessions, only the written data is — so it's worth having everything gathered before sitting down to fill it in completely.

What almost no one mentions: the wait isn't just bureaucratic, it's emotional

Waiting more than a year for an answer on whether your training “counts” here isn't just a slow procedure. For many people it touches directly on professional identity: years of study and experience are left in a kind of limbo, with no one — not the system, and sometimes not even the person themselves — quite sure what to do with them in the meantime. There's no way to speed this up that doesn't depend on the administration, and pretending there's a magic shortcut doesn't help anyone.

The law sets a 6-month deadline to resolve applications, but in practice it's common for it to take considerably longer. The more complete and clear the documentation from the start, the fewer reasons the administration will have to request clarifications that delay the process further.

If the decision ends up being unfavourable, it isn't the end of the road: you can file a complaint with the Ministry itself within one month of notification, and if that complaint doesn't succeed either, there's an appeal to the administrative courts. It's not a path anyone would choose, but it exists.

What does help is not putting life on hold while you wait. Working in something else during that time isn't a failure or a step backwards — it's simply what needs to be done while a procedure moves at its own pace, which is almost never the pace you'd want.

What the official channels don't tell you: the community does

There's something telling in all this: not even the Ministry of Universities publishes data on how long recognitions actually take. What's known about real waiting times comes almost always from the people going through the process themselves, sharing it with each other.

During review, you may receive a requerimiento — a request for additional documentation the Ministry considers necessary. From the moment it's received, you have 10 working days to respond, with the possibility of requesting an extension if you need more time. Failing to sort it out in time can lead to the file being closed, although if that happens because a notification never arrived, you can file an appeal explaining it.

It's also common, especially in health professions, for them to ask for a certificate of continuous professional practice if six years or more have passed since the qualification was obtained — a document certifying your work experience, which also needs to be legalised and apostilled like the rest.

Since the Ministry doesn't provide this information officially, citizen communities and platforms have formed, made up of people going through the recognition process, where real waiting times, questions and concrete experiences are shared. They don't replace official information, but for day-to-day questions about the procedure they're often more useful than any institutional website.

Community resources

Besides the official sources, there are citizen-run platforms created by people who have been through the recognition process, where experiences, real waiting times and frequently asked questions are shared: homologacionjustaya.org. It isn't an official source, but it can be useful for day-to-day questions the administration doesn't always answer.
